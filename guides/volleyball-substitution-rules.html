Rules explained

Volleyball substitution rules explained

Six entries onto the court per team, per set, and a lock that ties every starter to one substitute for the whole set. Miss either half of that and a scorer will wave through a substitution the rules do not actually allow.

The six-entry cap

Each team gets six substitutions per set. Once those six are used, the team cannot make another substitution for the rest of that set, no matter which players are involved or how much of the set is left to play. The cap resets for the next set.

Entries, not pairs, is what the cap counts

The easiest mistake to make when tracking substitutions is counting swaps instead of entries. The cap counts every time a player enters the court, not every time two players trade places. A starter who is subbed off and later comes back on uses up two of the team's six entries: one when the substitute goes on, and a second when the starter returns to replace them. A pair that only ever swaps once, with the substitute finishing the set on court, uses only one.

The pair lock: one starter, one substitute

A starter can only be replaced by one specific substitute for that set, and that substitute can only be replaced by that same starter. Once the pairing exists, it is fixed for the rest of the set.

The rule that matters

A starter may only be replaced by one specific substitute for that set, and that substitute may only be replaced by that starter. Once the pairing is set, no third player can swap in for either of them, no matter how many entries the team has left.

The lock follows the player, not the position

Rotation moves every player between the six numbered court positions on every side-out, so a pair lock tied to a position rather than to the two specific players would misfire the first time either of them rotated. Locking the pair to player identity is what lets the rule keep working correctly no matter how many times the lineup has rotated since the substitution was made.

A pair is worth two entries, and no more

The pair lock is often described as if a starter and their substitute could then trade places freely for the rest of the set. They cannot. A starter may leave the court once and come back once; their substitute may enter once. That is two entries, and then both of those players are done for the set — so a team wanting all six entries needs at least three separate pairs.

The libero is outside this system

Libero replacements are not substitutions. They do not create a pair, and they never count against the six-entry cap — a team can send its libero on and off many times in a set without touching its substitution total at all. More on libero rules →

A worked example: burning the cap

Only three players ever left the bench — #9, #14 and #11 — yet the team used all six entries, because every return to the court counts the same as every trip off it. Three pairs is also the minimum that can reach six, since no pair is ever worth more than two.

Common questions

How many substitutions can a volleyball team make in a set?

Six entries onto the court per team, per set. That is not the same as six different players. A starter who goes off and later comes back on uses up two of those six entries, one for each time a player enters the court.

Can the same substitute replace the same starter more than once in a set?

No, and this is where the pair lock is stricter than it first looks. A starter may leave the court once and come back once, and their substitute may enter once — so a starter-and-substitute pair is worth two entries in total, after which both of those players are finished for that set. The lock separately means a team cannot send a different player in for either of them. Reaching all six entries therefore needs at least three different pairs.

Does a libero replacement count as one of a team's substitutions?

No. Libero replacements run on a completely separate system with no cap and no pair lock. They never use up any of a team's six substitution entries.
