Volleyball rotation explained

Six players, six numbered positions, and one rule that decides when everybody moves. Rotation confuses new players and new scorers for the same reason: the numbers go one way and the players walk the other, and the rotation does not happen every time you win a point.

The six positions

Every player on court stands in one of six numbered positions. The numbering is fixed and never changes — what changes is which player is standing in each one.

Positions 2, 3 and 4 are the front row; 5, 6 and 1 are the back row. That split matters more than the individual numbers, because it is what decides who may attack from where and who may block.

Which way the rotation goes

Rotation is clockwise when you look at the court from above with the net at the top. Each player moves into the position that was to their clockwise side:

2 (front right)1 (back right)becomes the new server
1 (back right)6 (back centre)leaves the serve
6 (back centre)5 (back left)stays back row
5 (back left)4 (front left)moves up to the net
4 (front left)3 (front centre)stays front row
3 (front centre)2 (front right)one rotation from serving

Here is the part that trips everyone up. The numbers advance clockwise, but the players appear to walk anticlockwise around the court — front-left goes to front-centre, front-centre to front-right, then down the right side to the serve and back along the rear. Both descriptions are the same movement. If you find the word "clockwise" unhelpful, ignore it and memorise one thing instead: the front-right player becomes the next server.

When a rotation actually happens

The rule that matters

A team rotates only when it wins the serve back from the opponent — a side-out. Winning a point while you are already serving does not rotate anybody.

So a team that serves eight points in a row rotates once, at the start of that run, and the same player serves all eight. A team that loses the serve, wins it back, loses it and wins it back rotates twice. Over a set, your rotation count equals your number of side-outs, not your number of points.

This also fixes the serving order for the whole set. Once the starting lineup is submitted, the order in which those six players serve cannot change for that set, no matter how the play goes. Only a substitution changes who occupies a slot in that order.

Positional faults and rotational faults

Two different faults get confused, and they are penalised at different moments.

Positional fault

At the exact instant the server strikes the ball, every player must be in their correct relative position. Each back-row player must have at least part of a foot further from the net than their corresponding front-row player, and within each row the left, centre and right order must hold. It is about relative positions, not exact spots on the floor — nobody has to stand on a mark.

The moment the ball is struck, all of that stops applying and players may move anywhere on their own side. That is why a setter can sprint to front-right on every single ball.

Rotational fault

A rotational fault is serving out of order — the wrong player takes the serve for that turn. It is a fault against the serving team: the opponent gets the point and the serve, and the rotation is corrected to what it should have been.

One lasting restriction

A back-row player may not attack the ball above the height of the net from in front of the attack line. That restriction survives the serve — unlike positional order, it applies for the whole rally, which is why the back-row rules keep mattering after the whistle.

Where the libero fits

The libero is a back-row specialist who comes on and off without using a substitution. A libero replacement is not a substitution and never affects the rotation order — the libero simply occupies the slot of the player they replaced, and that player takes the slot back later. The libero may not serve in most competitions, may not complete an attack above net height, and may not set the ball with the hands from in front of the attack line for a teammate to attack above net height.

Because the libero occupies a rotation slot rather than joining the cycle, a scorer tracking rotation has to keep libero replacements separate from substitutions. Mixing them is how a sheet ends up with the wrong server. More on libero rules →

Common questions

Which way does volleyball rotation go?

Clockwise, seen from above with the net at the top of the picture. The player in position 2 moves to position 1 and becomes the server, 1 moves to 6, 6 to 5, 5 to 4, 4 to 3 and 3 to 2. It feels counter-intuitive because the players themselves walk anticlockwise around the court, but the position numbers advance clockwise.

Do you rotate every time you win a point?

No. You rotate only when you win the serve back from the other team, which is called a side-out. If you were already serving and you win the point, everyone stays exactly where they are and the same player serves again. This is the single most common misunderstanding about rotation.

Can players move out of position once the ball is served?

Yes. The positions only have to be correct at the instant the server strikes the ball. After that everyone is free to move anywhere on their own side, which is why the setter can run to the front-right on every ball regardless of where they rotated from. The one lasting restriction is that a back-row player may not attack the ball above the height of the net from in front of the attack line.
