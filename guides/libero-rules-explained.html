Libero rules explained

A libero replacement is not a substitution. It skips the six-entry cap entirely, it is never locked to a pair, and a team can use it as often as the game requires — inside a set of restrictions on what the libero is allowed to do once they are actually on court.

A specialist in a different shirt

The libero is a back-row defensive specialist, easy to spot because they wear a shirt that contrasts with the rest of the team's kit. The role exists for reception and defence rather than attacking, and the rules around it are built to keep it that way.

A replacement, not a substitution

Bringing the libero on or off court is called a replacement, and it runs on a separate system from ordinary substitutions. Replacements are not limited by the six-entry cap, they do not create a locked pair the way a starter and substitute do, and a team can make as many of them as the match requires.

The rule that matters

A libero replacement is unlimited and uncapped, but it still has to happen with the ball out of play and before the whistle for the next serve. It also only ever replaces a back-row player — the libero never takes a front-row slot.

One replacement per dead ball

A libero replacement is instantaneous rather than a process, so the real rule is not that one has to finish before the next begins — it is that a completed rally must happen between two libero replacements. A team cannot swap the libero out and a different player in during the same stoppage; the ball has to be played first.

What the libero may not do

The trade-off for the unlimited replacements is a set of restrictions that apply only to the libero, all pointing the same direction: keep the role defensive.

  • May not serve, in most competitions — this is one of the rules that varies most by competition, so check your own league or federation before assuming it either way.
  • May not block, or even attempt to block. This is the most widely known libero restriction and it has no competition variance worth hedging — it applies at the net and it applies to the attempt, not just to a completed block.
  • May not complete an attack above the height of the net, anywhere on the court.
  • May not set the ball with their hands from in front of the attack line for a teammate to then attack the ball above net height.

Common questions

Is a libero replacement the same as a substitution?

No. A libero replacement does not count against a team's six substitution entries and is not locked to a starter and substitute pair. It is a separate mechanism that only swaps the libero for a back-row player, and back again, as many times as the match requires.

Can the libero serve?

In most competitions, no. Some competitions do allow the libero to serve in one rotation, so this is one of the rules worth checking against your own league or federation before a match.

What can the libero not do on the court?

The libero cannot block or attempt to block, cannot complete an attack above the height of the net anywhere on court, and cannot set the ball with their hands from in front of the attack line for a teammate to then attack above net height. In most competitions the libero also cannot serve, though that is the rule that varies most, so check your own league. The restrictions all exist because the libero is a defensive specialist, not an attacking one.
